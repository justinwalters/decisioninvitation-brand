# Art direction

**Direction:** Open frame. **Brand idea:** Catalyst of analysis. **Status:** visual proposal.

## A held question with a way through

The graphic system starts with a clear boundary and one opening. The boundary gives analysis a useful scope; the opening gives the eye a direction to continue. A perspective sits inside or alongside the frame. A condition or next move appears beyond the opening.

This is a visual metaphor, not a literal depiction of security or a product engine. A broken boundary must never suggest a data leak, an unfinished load, or an unvalidated field. Use it for editorial composition; actual forms use complete, conventional controls.

## Graphic construction

Build frames from right angles and a single controlled curve where the composition calls for it. Use a 1–2 px rule in ordinary web editorial work and scale proportionally in print. The opening usually occupies 16–24% of one side. Keep at least one body-text line of space between the content and the frame.

Use one principal opening per frame and usually one principal frame per composition. Aim the opening toward the next argument or generous empty space. Do not fill the opening with an arrowhead. Do not nest frames indefinitely or join them into a decorative maze.

The monogram's D construction is its own locked source geometry. Frame artwork can echo its balance without stretching or cropping the actual mark to make a border.

## The catalyst sequence

An editorial sequence can show three fields:

1. **Question:** what choice is in front of the owner?
2. **Perspective:** what reason or condition changes its shape?
3. **Next move:** decide, resolve a condition, or name the missing fact.

Use typography and space to make the change legible. Give the next move a place of equal or greater clarity than the initial question. This keeps the system from glorifying endless contemplation. Illustrative sequences carry an “Illustrative example” label and never masquerade as a live decision record.

## Composition

Use asymmetric relationships rather than arbitrary imbalance. A large statement may occupy seven columns while a precise note occupies four, separated by a deliberate interval. Anchor related elements to a shared edge or baseline. A quiet margin is useful territory; it does not need to be filled with decoration.

Paper dominates reading. Mist separates a chapter or specimen. Ink creates a decisive opening or closing field. Green supports an image or a single editorial section. Orange remains a small punctuation in the overall composition. Never turn every panel into the signature's full palette.

## Photography

Select images with a reason to exist: a person considering a prepared brief, a team member inspecting a plan, a working surface with visible use, a pause between reading and replying. Natural light and credible materials are preferred. People may be absorbed, alert, uncertain, or attentive; they need not perform triumph.

Crop close enough to identify the work without exposing confidential content. Reduce visual noise in the crop rather than adding a heavy brand-color overlay. Preserve skin tones. Use a Paper or Mist surround when the image palette is busy.

Avoid posed handshake stock, smiling-at-laptop shorthand, boardroom clichés, robots, glowing brains, abstract particles, and synthetic “AI” light. Do not present generated people as employees or customers. Clearly label illustrative imagery where the context could imply documentary evidence.

## Illustration and information graphics

Use flat planes, measured rules, open frames, and a small number of labeled objects. Illustrations should explain a relation: separate perspectives, a changed condition, a boundary, or an accountable next step. Use Ink for outlines and labels, Mist/Paper for fields, and Orange only for the point of change.

Avoid pie charts or weighted scores without actual supporting data. Do not illustrate analysis as a magical blender producing the correct decision. Attribution and uncertainty remain visible when they matter to the story.

## Icons

Use a coherent 24-unit outline grid, 1.5–2-unit strokes, simple geometry, and consistent optical weight. Pair unfamiliar symbols with text. The Di mark never substitutes for a submit, decision, completion, security, or AI icon. New icon sources need a recorded license; do not silently mix library styles.

## Materials

Uncoated paper, matte finishes, and disciplined ink coverage suit the identity. A small orange point can be printed as a second color when practical. In blind embossing, laser engraving, or low-fidelity production, use the one-color mark and proof the D counter and i separation. Foil and metallic finishes are optional material treatments, never new brand colors.
